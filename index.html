Quick answers

How high should my dog's food bowl be?

No veterinary guideline sets a height. A common rule of thumb from pet-product guides puts the rim between your dog's elbow height and about 2 to 3 inches (5 to 7.5 cm) below it. A floor bowl is fine for most healthy dogs, so a raised bowl is a comfort choice. For large and giant breeds, a study found the bloat risk depended on whether the bowl was above or below 1 ft (30.5 cm); see the next answer.

Are raised bowls safe for large dogs?

It depends on the height and on how big the breed is. A study followed 1,637 dogs of 11 large and giant breeds and asked whether the bowl was on the floor, raised 1 ft (30.5 cm) or less, or raised more than 1 ft [1][2]. In this study, large meant breeds of 23 to 45 kg (50 to 99 lb) and giant meant over 45 kg (99 lb) [10]. Bloat (GDV) cases per 1,000 dog-years:

  • Large breeds: floor 18, raised 1 ft or less 56 (3.06 times the risk), raised more than 1 ft 29 (not a significant difference).
  • Giant breeds: floor 17, raised 1 ft or less 15 (not a significant difference), raised more than 1 ft 39 (2.29 times the risk).

So going by this study, a raised bowl for a large breed would sit above 30.5 cm and for a giant breed at 30.5 cm or lower. It is one study with 98 bloat cases split into small groups; a review says the height results could be chance, and the authors didn't explain them [2]. An internet survey with 1,114 bloat cases found no clear effect of bowl height, though it published no numbers on this [2][9]. Dogs with megaesophagus are fed from a raised position on purpose: front legs higher than back legs, then upright for at least 10 to 15 minutes after meals [8]. If you're unsure, ask your vet.

How tall should a cat's bowl be?

Most healthy cats eat fine from the floor. For an older or stiff cat, a small lift with the rim a little below the elbow can make meals easier. No study has measured this, so treat it as a comfort tweak.

How wide should a cat's bowl be? Is whisker fatigue real?

In a study of 38 cats, whisker-friendly dishes made no difference to time spent eating, food dropped or food eaten, though 63% of owners said their cat preferred the wider dish [3]. A wide, shallow dish at least as wide as your cat's whisker span is a cheap thing to try.

Do slow feeder bowls work?

Yes, at slowing a fast eater down. In a study of 10 pet dogs, meals took significantly longer from a slow feeder than from a plain bowl, and the dogs were still slower after nine days of practice [4]. Vet sources recommend slow feeders and puzzle feeders for three reasons:

  • Bloat (GDV) in big dogs: eating fast was a risk factor in two Purdue studies [1][12], and VCA Animal Hospitals advises feeding large and giant dogs from the floor with a puzzle feeder or slow-down bowl [11].
  • Gulping, vomiting and gas: Veterinary Partner, the Veterinary Information Network's site for pet owners, says small meals in feeders prevent gulping and may lessen vomiting in cats [16]. Its article on gas in dogs says dogs swallow more air when they feel they must eat quickly, and suggests puzzle feeding bowls to slow them down [17].
  • Cats: a feline vet consensus recommends frequent small meals from puzzle feeders, which it says increase activity and help weight control, and notes that cats that gorge may vomit [13]. In vet case reports, overweight cats lost 6 to 20% of their body weight with food puzzles [14].

Owners say the same in their own words, though these are personal reports, not research. In a 2026 Reddit thread about slow feeders, a reply with 170 upvotes says the poster's small dog throws up without one, and another says it "immediately stopped the problem" for a dog that gulped its food and vomited [18].

Mealtime also gets more interesting: in a small study of 4 dogs, a feeding toy made meals last longer and kept the dogs more active, and their stress hormone (cortisol) was lower after a week than on the first day [15]. No trial has yet tested whether a slow feeder prevents bloat itself; VCA lists it alongside smaller, more frequent meals [11].

How big should the bowl be?

Big enough for one meal with room to spare. We use 1.5 times the meal volume for a regular bowl and 2 times for a slow feeder, because the ridges take up space. 1 US cup is 236.6 ml. If you weigh your pet's food or feed it by the can, pick the food in the calculator and it turns grams, ounces or cans into volume. This is our rule of thumb.

How much should I feed my dog or cat?

Start with the chart on the food bag, or work it out: resting energy is 70 × (weight in kg)0.75 kcal a day, times a life-stage factor, for example 1.6 for a spayed or neutered adult dog, 1.2 for a spayed or neutered adult cat, and 2 to 3 for puppies and kittens [5]. Very active cats, like ones that go outdoors, need about a third more than indoor or neutered cats (100 against 75 kcal per kg0.67 a day) [32], so the calculator uses 1.6 for them instead of 1.2 [19]. Divide by the calories per cup on the bag (or, if you weigh food, by the calories per kg, times 1,000 for grams), then pick a bowl that holds one meal with room to spare. These are estimates and pets can differ by as much as 50%, so check body condition and adjust [5]. For weight loss, ask your vet for a plan.

What should I feed?

A food whose label says it is complete and balanced for your pet's life stage: growth, adult maintenance or all life stages. For a puppy that will be a large dog, the label should say the food includes growth of large size dogs [6]. That rule exists because large-breed puppies need tighter calcium limits [7].

Short legs, long ears or a flat face?

Short-legged dogs: measure the elbow height, because size charts based on weight don't account for leg length. Long-eared dogs often dip their ears in the bowl, so a narrower opening or a tilted bowl helps. Flat-faced pets usually find wide, shallow or tilted bowls easier. These are practical tips, not study results.
