# Pet nutrition and bowl size

1. Ask for: dog or cat; elbow height (floor to elbow, pet standing on a flat floor); what the pet eats in one meal: each food and how much, in US cups, grams, ounces or cans (nobody needs to weigh a cup of food: `foods.js` has the grams per cup of 69 foods, and a type for foods that aren't listed); whether the dog is a large breed (adults 23–45 kg, 50–99 lb) or a giant breed (over 45 kg, 99 lb); and whether the pet eats very fast, has short legs and a long back, long ears, a flat face, or is older. For cats the whisker span is optional.
2. Work it out, or run `bowlfit.js`:
   - Bowl height, from the floor to the top edge of the bowl: from elbow height down to 7.5 cm (3 in) below it. Compare it with how tall a bowl is; don't call it "rim", which people read as the wall or the inside depth.
   - Large breed: a floor bowl, or a bowl whose top edge is above 30.5 cm (1 ft) and up to elbow height. If the elbow is 30.5 cm or lower, floor only.
   - Giant breed: a floor bowl, or a bowl whose top edge is no higher than 30.5 cm (1 ft), even when the elbow rule says higher.
   - 1 ft = 12 in × 2.54 = 30.48 cm, shown as 30.5 cm. The cut-off comes from the one study that measured bowl height (see "Which bowl", point 3).
   - Capacity: meal volume × 1.5, or × 2 for a slow feeder, rounded up to the next 50 ml. Meal volume: cups × 236.6 ml; grams, ounces (× 28.35 g) or cans (the can's net weight) ÷ the food's grams in one cup × 236.6 ml, added up over the foods. Grams in one cup (`foods.js`): dry food = the maker's kcal per cup ÷ kcal per kg × 1,000 (or the cup weight on the label); dry food that isn't listed 86.1 g, the 10th percentile of the listed ones; wet food 236.6 g (1.0 g per ml, a little light on purpose so the bowl isn't too small); fresh-cooked food from the maker (JustFoodForDogs, Nom Nom and Freshpet: 146.5 to 188 g), or 146.5 g when it isn't listed, which raw and homemade food use too; freeze-dried 45 g; water or broth 236.6 g.
   - Cats: a dish at least as wide as the whisker span.
   - What the pet eats now, if the owner gives amounts: each food's amount × its calories × meals a day (`fedKcalDay`), next to the calories a day. Lead with this nutrition part; the bowl comes after.
   - Water or broth mixed in (`id: "water"`) counts as 0 kcal. If the owner says which broth and roughly how much, look its calories up online yourself: USDA FoodData Central branded foods (https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/), or the maker's label. First check that what they named really is a broth. If it is, pass its calories on that row as `kcal100` (kcal per 100 ml of ready-to-serve broth, not powder or concentrate), and say which entry you used with its link. If you can't identify it as a broth, say so and ask for the calories in one cup of their homemade broth (`id: "homemade-broth"`, `kcal100` = kcal per cup ÷ 2.366).
   - Our bowls (`PRODUCTS` in `bowlfit.js`): name the ones that may fit (say "may fit", not "fit"), and re-rank them whenever a number changes. If none fits, name the three closest and say roughly how far each is off (height, capacity, width), gently, as a reference ("a bit shorter than the suggested 12.2 cm", "~150 ml less"); never answer with no bowl. Before the owner gives any numbers, name our six best sellers in this order: lilac plate, two-tone raised flower, Pink Planet, lilac pedestal, sage green, butter yellow. The Pink Planet plate (21.6 cm across, for medium to large dogs) holds about 650 ml, our estimate from its size, so say "about". Large breeds: flat plates only, since raised bowls of 1 ft or less carried 3.06 times the bloat risk.
   - Food per meal, if the owner doesn't know it: resting energy (RER) = 70 × (weight in kg)^0.75 kcal a day, times a factor for life stage and activity (dog: 1.6 spayed or neutered adult, 1.8 intact, 1.2–1.4 not very active, about 1.9 active adult, 2.0 working detection dog, 2–3 puppy; cat: 1.2, 1.4, 1.0, about 1.6 very active or outdoors, 2–3), divided by the kcal per cup on the bag (for grams: kcal a day ÷ kcal per kg × 1,000) and by the number of meals. Say it is a starting point and to adjust to body condition (Pet Nutrition Alliance; Merck; National Research Council 2006; Mullis et al., PeerJ 2015; FEDIAF 2025, Table VII-9: active cats 100 against 75 kcal/kg^0.67 for indoor or neutered cats, a third more). Racing sled dogs need far more, about 9,000 to 13,800 kcal a day in the Yukon Quest (Loftus et al., 2014).
   - If the owner mentions something the vet is watching, say what vet sources advise, in gentle words (say "kidney values to watch", not "kidney disease"): kidney values (chronic kidney disease), a renal diet from stage 2 (low phosphorus, modest high-quality protein; Merck); liver values, most need no fat cut, protein is cut only with hepatic encephalopathy, and copper build-up needs a low-copper liver diet (Merck); pancreatitis, dogs under 20 g fat per 1,000 kcal, cats moderately less fat (Merck); high triglycerides or cholesterol, no more than 24 g fat per 1,000 kcal (Weeth, Today's Veterinary Practice 2022); overweight, start at 60% of the calories for dogs or 70% for cats and lose about 1% of body weight a week (Merck). The vet's own plan comes first.
3. Say how solid each number is. Bowl height and capacity are rules of thumb. The 1 ft cut-off for large and giant breeds comes from one cohort study of 1,637 dogs with 98 bloat cases (Glickman et al., JAVMA 2000; numbers as summarised by Buckley, Veterinary Evidence 2017), so call it a guide, not proof; an internet survey of 2,551 dogs found no clear effect of bowl height (Pipan et al., JAVMA 2012). Whisker-friendly dishes made no measurable difference in a 38-cat study (Slovak and Foster, 2021).
4. Don't diagnose. If the pet has a medical condition, tell the owner to follow their vet's feeding plan.
5. In code: `node -e "console.log(require('./bowlfit.js').recommend({species:'dog',unit:'cm',elbow:30,cupsPerMeal:1,fastEater:true}))"`. With foods: `recommend({species:'dog',elbow:30,foods:[{id:'hills-sd-adult-chicken-barley-dry',amount:100,unit:'g'}]})`; `unit` is `g`, `oz`, `cups` or `unit` (one can, pouch or tray), and the ids are in `foods.js`.

## Which bowl: slow feeder or regular, raised or not, and what material

Use this when someone asks whether their dog or cat needs a slow feeder or a regular bowl, a raised bowl or not, or which material to pick (ceramic, stainless steel such as 304, titanium or plastic). Give the reason and say how solid it is. The tags in brackets point to the sources at the end of this section.

1. **A plain bowl on the floor is the default.** We found no veterinary guideline that says a healthy dog or cat needs a slow feeder or a raised bowl. Size the bowl to the meal (see capacity above) and wash it with soap and hot water after each use [FDA-handling].
2. **Slow feeder: for dogs and cats that gulp.** It slows the meal down. Vet sources recommend it for big dogs at risk of bloat, for pets that gulp and then vomit, and, as puzzle feeders, for cats (point 6).
   - Speed: 10 pet dogs (1–12 years, 4.5–30 kg) ate half their daily ration from a plain bowl and from a slow feeder for 9 days each. With the slow feeder they took longer to finish (P = 0.006) and ate at a lower rate (P = 0.002). They got quicker with practice (P = 0.027) but stayed slower than at the plain bowl [Buckley 2016]. In a two-way choice test they went to the plain bowl first, so the authors warn against selling slow eating as a game the dog enjoys; the two bowls also differed in colour [Buckley 2016].
   - Bloat (GDV): eating fast was a risk factor in a Purdue case-control study of 101 cases and in the Purdue cohort of 1,637 dogs [Glickman 1997] [Glickman 2000], and Merck lists rapid eating among factors linked to GDV [Merck-GDV]. VCA Animal Hospitals lists eating very quickly as a GDV risk factor and advises feeding large and giant dogs from the floor with a puzzle feeder or "slow-down bowl", along with smaller, more frequent meals [VCA-large]. A study of 74 Irish setters with bloat found no link with eating speed [Elwood 1998], and no trial has tested whether a slow feeder prevents bloat.
   - Gulping and vomiting: Veterinary Partner, VIN's site for pet owners, says small meals in feeders prevent gulping and may lessen vomiting, in a cat article by a veterinary behavior technician [VP-cats]. Its article on gas in dogs says dogs swallow more air when they feel they must eat quickly, and lists commercial puzzle feeding bowls as a way to slow them down [VP-gas].
   - Owner reports (personal accounts, not research): in a 2026 r/dogs thread on slow feeders with 166 comments, a reply with 170 upvotes says the poster's small dog throws up without one, and another says it "immediately stopped the problem" for a dog that gulped its food and vomited [Reddit-dogs-2026].
   - Mealtime enrichment: 4 dogs fed from a feeding toy spent longer eating and were more active than with a steel bowl, and their cortisol was lower on day 7 than on day 1 [Boonhoh 2023]. 38 pet dogs were willing to work for food from a snuffle mat even with free food next to it, though they didn't prefer it [Rothkoff 2024].
   - Choose ridges the dog can manage. Flat-faced dogs usually do better with low, shallow ones. This is a practical tip, not a study result.
   - Give it about twice the meal volume, because the ridges take up space (our rule of thumb, see capacity above).
3. **Raised bowls for large or giant breeds: go by height.** The one study that measured bowl height found the extra bloat risk at different heights for large and giant breeds. Use it to recommend a height, say where it comes from, and let the owner decide with their vet.
   - The study followed 1,637 dogs of 11 large and giant breeds. Owners said whether the bowl was not raised, raised 1 ft (30.5 cm) or less, or raised more than 1 ft. Large means the study's 7 breeds of 23–45 kg (50–99 lb), giant its 4 breeds over 45 kg (99 lb) [Glickman 2000] [Glickman 2000-breeds]. Overall, a raised bowl was linked to about 20% of bloat cases in the large breeds and 52% in the giant breeds [Glickman 2000], but the risky height differed by size. Bloat cases per 1,000 dog-years (95% range), from the review's summary of the paper [Buckley 2017-raised]:

     | Breed size | Floor | Raised 1 ft (30.5 cm) or less | Raised more than 1 ft |
     |---|---|---|---|
     | Large, 23–45 kg | 18 (12–24), 625 dogs | 56 (26–86), 105 dogs: 3.06 times the risk (1.60–5.87, P = 0.002) | 29 (−11 to 47), 144 dogs: not a significant difference |
     | Giant, over 45 kg | 17 (7–27), 341 dogs | 15 (−6 to 36), 60 dogs: not a significant difference | 39 (25–53), 324 dogs: 2.29 times the risk (1.15–4.58, P = 0.02) |

   - Recommendation: a floor bowl, or a raised bowl above 30.5 cm for a large breed and at 30.5 cm or lower for a giant breed.
   - How solid: 98 bloat cases split into small groups, so the review says the height results could be chance; the authors didn't explain why the risky height differs by size; bowl height was asked once at the start and dogs were followed for up to about five years [Buckley 2017-raised]. Not significant isn't the same as no risk: large breeds above 1 ft had 29 cases against 18 on the floor, but the range was too wide to call it a difference. The questionnaire asked for "the height of the food bowl" without saying rim or stand; this tool compares the height to the top edge of the bowl.
   - Other evidence: an internet survey with 1,114 bloat cases found no clear effect of bowl height, but it published no numbers on this. A review of both studies called their findings conflicting, found none showing that a raised bowl lowers the risk, and advised floor feeding for at-risk dogs [Pipan 2012] [Buckley 2017-raised].
   - Merck's advice for owners of high-risk breeds: avoid raised bowls, feed several small meals instead of one big one, limit exercise before and after meals and keep mealtimes calm [Merck-GDV]. Cornell's canine health center also says to avoid raised bowls [Cornell-GDV].
4. **When a raised bowl helps, with the vet's plan.**
   - Megaesophagus: the dog eats with its front legs higher than its back legs and stays that way for at least 10–15 minutes after eating, so gravity carries the food to the stomach. Frequent small meals work best for most dogs [Merck-ME]. A raised bowl alone isn't enough. In 12 dogs fed upright in Bailey chairs, food cleared at very different speeds, and 9 of them had their food texture, time upright or medicine changed after an X-ray video test [Haines 2019].
   - Arthritis in the front legs, shoulders or neck: an arthritis site founded by a board-certified veterinary surgeon and rehabilitation specialist suggests raising the bowl to about shoulder height, so the neck stays level and less weight goes on the front legs [CARE]. This is expert advice, not a trial result. Shoulder height is higher than this tool's height rule (elbow height down to 7.5 cm below it), so let the vet or rehab vet set it (for a large or giant breed, see point 3).
5. **Material: ceramic, stainless steel, titanium or plastic.**
   - How the bowl is washed matters more than what it's made of. On 68 dog dishes, a week of FDA's bowl hygiene steps cut bacteria, and washing in water hotter than 71 °C (160 °F) beat cold or lukewarm water [Luisana 2022]. Two minutes in warm soapy water plus scrubbing fully removed ringworm spores from metal, plastic and glass bowls [Moriello 2019].
   - Studies on material disagree. In 96 dog bowls from Italian homes, metal bowls carried more bacteria than plastic ones, and bowls used for wet food more than dry [Raspa 2023]. In a 14-day test of new water bowls, total bacteria didn't differ significantly by material; plastic ended with the highest average count, and the ceramic bowl had the most kinds of bacteria. That was a conference poster [Wright 2018].
   - Ceramic: the glaze is what touches the food. FDA says lead in pottery may leach into food, and it keeps an import alert for lead and cadmium in ceramicware [FDA-lead]. In lab tests of 18 glazes, acids and higher temperatures increased how much metal came out [Demont 2012]. Pick ceramic sold as food safe, with a lead and cadmium release test.
   - Stainless steel: ask which grade it is, such as 304. Type 201 is a stainless steel with less nickel and more manganese (3.5–5.5% nickel, 5.5–7.5% manganese), first made to save nickel, and its maker rates it for mild environments [CLF-201]. We found no study comparing pet bowls by steel grade.
   - Titanium: we found no study on titanium pet bowls, and nothing showing they are safer for pets than stainless steel or glazed ceramic.
6. **Cats.**
   - The AAFP feeding consensus says to split the day's food into several small meals and use puzzle feeders when possible, because they increase activity and help weight control. It also notes that cats that gorge may then vomit. Two of its six authors consult for pet food companies [AAFP 2018].
   - Case reports from vet behaviour practices describe weight loss and calmer cats after food puzzles were added along with other changes. Overweight cats lost from 6.4% of body weight in 3.5 months to 20% in 12 months. These are case reports, not a trial [Dantas 2016].
   - In a randomised trial of 92 cats over four weeks, attention-seeking fell both with food puzzles and with toys [Hoff 2026] (as summarised in a 2026 review in Animals). In a 44-cat weight-loss trial a ball-type food toy didn't change owner-rated quality of life [Dodd 2021], and 17 cats offered both ate more from a tray than from a puzzle [Delgado 2022], so introduce puzzles gradually.
   - Whisker-friendly wide dishes made no difference to time spent eating, food dropped or food eaten in 38 cats, though 24 of the owners (63%) said their cat preferred the wider dish [Slovak 2021].
   - We found no study on raised or tilted bowls for cats.
7. **Signs that need a vet, not a new bowl.** Retching without bringing anything up, drooling, restlessness or a swelling belly can mean bloat (GDV), which is an emergency [Merck-GDV]. Bringing food back up can be regurgitation, the main sign of megaesophagus [Merck-ME], so see a vet before changing bowls. Regurgitation is not eating fast: it is passive, with no effort from the belly, often undigested food from the food pipe [Merck-vomit], so a slow feeder is not the answer; feeding from higher up and smaller meals more often are [Merck-ME] [Merck-esophagitis]. Being sick or licking the lips on an empty stomach, often at night or early morning, is different again: lip licking can be a sign of nausea [Merck-nausea], and in a study of 20 dogs vets used more frequent meals, a late-evening meal and medicine, and 12 of the 17 that kept the diagnosis improved [Ferguson 2016]. It is a diagnosis of exclusion, so the vet rules out other causes first.

## Which kind of food: dry, wet, freeze-dried, fresh, raw or homemade

Use this when someone asks what type of food to feed a dog or whether to add water to kibble. Give the reason and say how solid it is. The tags in brackets point to the sources at the end of this section.

1. **Label first, format second.** The main food should say it is complete and balanced for the dog's life stage. In the US that is an AAFCO statement for growth, adult maintenance or all life stages [AAFCO]. In the EU it is "complete" [FEDIAF]. In China it is 全价 or 宠物配合饲料 with the species and life stage, and treats must say 宠物零食 [MARA]. Foods for intermittent or supplementary feeding, toppers and treats should stay at 10% of the diet or less [WSAVA-select]. A puppy that will weigh 70 lb (32 kg) or more as an adult needs a growth food that includes large-size dogs [Merck].
2. **Ask the maker.** WSAVA suggests asking whether the company employs a nutritionist with a PhD in animal nutrition or ACVN/ECVCN board certification, who formulates the food, what quality control it runs and how many calories it has. Be cautious with a brand that won't answer [WSAVA-select].
3. **Dry or wet.** For most healthy dogs either works, and so does a mix. Choose by the dog's needs, the budget and the routine [Tufts-canned]. Dry food is about 10% water and canned food about 68–78% [Merck]. China's standard calls food under 14% water dry and 60% or more wet [GB/T 31216]. So compare foods per 1000 kcal or on a dry-matter basis, not by the label percentages [Tufts-converter].
   - Water: in a 10-dog crossover run by a fresh-food company, dogs on a 71% moisture food drank less but took in 88 g more water a day than on kibble [Sires 2025].
   - Weight: wet food is not a proven weight tool. When kibble was soaked to 72% water and offered freely, 46 dogs ate more and fully made up the calories [Alexander 2017].
   - Teeth: plain kibble means slightly less plaque and tartar, and soaked kibble loses that effect. For a real dental benefit, look for a VOHC seal and keep brushing [VOHC] [Tufts-canned].
   - Cost: for a 55 lb dog, all-wet feeding cost about 5 times dry, and fresh food 4–6 times the priciest dry, in a 2022 price check [Tufts-cost].
   - Storage: refrigerate an opened can and use it within 48–72 hours. Use an opened bag within 2–3 months [WSAVA-FAQ] [Tufts-storage].
4. **Freeze-dried: treat it as raw unless the pack says it was cooked.** None of 49 freeze-dried cat products in a Cornell survey was fully cooked before freeze-drying, and few said raw on the pack; no dog survey was found [Reboul 2025]. Freezing, freeze-drying and dehydrating only reduce germs [CDC] [Merck], and high-pressure processing (HPP) is not sterile [AAHA-raw]. Hong Kong's government guidance treats freeze-dried raw as raw food and says to avoid it for puppies, older, pregnant or immunocompromised dogs unless a vet directs [AFCD 2026].
5. **Raw.** AVMA discourages feeding raw or undercooked animal protein [AVMA], AAHA does not endorse raw or dehydrated nonsterilized foods [AAHA-raw], and CDC does not recommend raw pet food [CDC].
   - FDA testing found Salmonella in 15 and Listeria in 32 of 196 raw samples, against 2 positives in 480 dry or semimoist samples [Nemser 2014].
   - Dogs can carry Salmonella without looking sick: 7 of 16 shed it after one contaminated meal [Finley 2007]. Raw-fed dogs carried more multidrug-resistant E. coli, 25% against 4% [Groat 2022].
   - 60% of 95 BARF rations analysed in Germany had at least one imbalance [Dillitzer 2011].
   - Claimed benefits come from owner surveys, such as a 4,022-dog questionnaire, partly funded by raw-food makers, whose authors say it does not prove cause [Hemida 2021].
   - Don't feed raw in a home with young children or older or immunocompromised people [WSAVA-FAQ]. In Los Angeles, 19 cats fell ill after raw milk, raw meat or raw pet food; 9 tested positive for bird flu (H5N1) and 14 died or were put down [CDC-MMWR 2026]. Dogs usually show mild signs [FDA-H5N1].
6. **Fresh-cooked commercial.** Fine if it passes step 1. No long-term health studies were found; the short trials are small and often run by the makers [Sires 2025]. It costs about 4–6 times the priciest dry food [Tufts-cost]. If fat matters, for example after pancreatitis, compare fat per 1000 kcal [Tufts-converter].
7. **Homemade.** Only with a recipe from a board-certified veterinary nutritionist, updated every year [AAHA-home]. 95% of 200 published recipes fell short on at least one nutrient [Stockman 2013], so did all 106 Portuguese-language recipes in another study [Pedrinelli 2017], and only 13% of owners followed their prescribed recipe exactly [Johnson 2016].
8. **Grain-free or pulse-heavy.** FDA's last update (December 2022) said the reports don't establish cause [FDA-DCM]. AAHA calls avoiding grain-free or legume-heavy diets the most conservative approach [AAHA 2021]. Tufts (June 2026) links diet-associated heart disease (DCM) to pulse-heavy diets, grain-free or not, with the cause still unknown [Tufts-DCM].
9. **Adding water to kibble.**
   - It helps when the dog needs more water. For calcium oxalate, urate or cystine bladder stones, the ACVIM consensus recommends food over 75% water or enough water added to kibble, aiming for a urine specific gravity of 1.020 or lower [ACVIM 2016]. Soft food also suits dogs with sore or missing teeth [Tufts-canned].
   - It doesn't improve digestion. 20 beagle puppies on kibble soaked at 1.5 ml of water per gram for 21 days digested it no better, grew no differently and had higher cortisol (one food, 10 dogs a group) [Zhang 2022]. It won't help weight loss on its own, because dogs eat more to make up for it [Alexander 2017].
   - Large and giant breeds: moistening alone wasn't linked to bloat (GDV), but moistened kibble with citric acid in its ingredients was, with an odds ratio of 4.19, in a 106-case study [Raghavan 2002]. Check the ingredient list, and feed two or more meals a day, since one meal a day and fast eating raised the risk [Glickman 1997] [Raghavan 2004]. A later review rated the evidence on kibble and bloat as insufficient [Buckley 2017].
   - Soaked kibble spoils like wet food. Pick up leftovers and refrigerate or throw them out promptly [FDA-handling]. No pet-specific time limit was found; CDC's rule for perishable human food is 2 hours, or 1 hour above 90°F (32°C) [CDC-food].
10. **Switching.** Mix the new food into the old over at least 3 days (25, 50, 75, then 100%) [WSAVA-FAQ], or over about a week, and longer for dogs with health problems or a sensitive stomach [Tufts-switch].
11. **Traditional Chinese veterinary medicine.** Practitioners describe kibble as warming and drying, and some advise against dry food in hot weather [Basko 2009] [Morgan 2017]. This is theory and clinical experience, not trial data. The classical texts, such as the Tang-dynasty 司牧安骥集 and the Ming-dynasty 元亨疗马集, were written for horses and stress regular, measured meals rather than any type of food [Si Mu An Ji Ji] [Yuan Heng Liao Ma Ji].
12. **When the vet should choose.** Raw, homemade or other unconventional diets, treats over 10% of calories, and a body condition score below 4 or above 5 out of 9 are all reasons for a fuller nutrition check by a vet [WSAVA 2011]. For a dog with a medical condition, the vet's feeding plan comes first.
